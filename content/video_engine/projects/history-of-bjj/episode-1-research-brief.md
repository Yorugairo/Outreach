# Episode 1 Research Brief — How Judo Became Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

## Thesis to test

Brazilian jiu-jitsu did not appear in one clean handoff. The evidence supports a
longer transformation: Kano systematized Kodokan judo in Japan; Japanese teachers,
immigrants, and performers carried overlapping “judo” and “jiu-jitsu” practices
abroad; and practitioners in Brazil adapted those practices within local sporting,
commercial, and political conditions.

This is a research thesis, not approved narration.

## Candidate chapter questions

1. What did Kano change when he founded the Kodokan in 1882?
2. Why did “judo” and “jiu-jitsu” remain overlapping public labels abroad?
3. What evidence places jiu-jitsu activity in Brazil before Maeda’s arrival?
4. What brought Maeda and other Japanese practitioners to Brazil?
5. What can the record actually establish about Carlos Gracie’s training?
6. Which parts of the familiar direct-handoff story are disputed or incomplete?
7. How did Brazilian institutions, public contests, nationalism, and promotion
   contribute to a locally distinct practice?
8. What is gained by describing BJJ as a transformation rather than an invention
   at a single moment?

## Source-quality priorities

1. Contemporary records and archives with precise locators.
2. Kodokan, university, museum, and federation institutional histories.
3. Peer-reviewed historical scholarship with accessible notes and bibliography.
4. Authoritative secondary works used with explicit limitations.

Promotional lineage pages, unsourced community histories, consultant assertions,
and reference videos may suggest a question but cannot approve a claim.

## Contested register

- Whether Maeda and Satake were the first judo/jiu-jitsu teachers in Brazil.
- The precise dates, duration, and directness of Carlos Gracie’s instruction under
  Maeda versus senior student Jacyntho Ferro.
- Whether a single lineage can adequately explain Brazilian jiu-jitsu’s emergence.
- The degree to which later promotional accounts compress multiple Japanese and
  Brazilian contributors into one founding narrative.

No direct quotation is required for the first script. Removing quotations reduces
locator risk without weakening the episode.
