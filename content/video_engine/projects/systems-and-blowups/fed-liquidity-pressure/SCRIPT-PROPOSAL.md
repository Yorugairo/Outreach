# The Hidden Fed Metric Breaking the Economy

Proposed narration v1 — 2026-09-18. Money Physics; one chapter in the broader economy playlist. User's title retained. One subscribe CTA, after the payoff. Scene headings are production labels, not spoken. Numbers and limitations are documented in SOURCES-AND-RESEARCH.md. This is an editorial proposal, not a recorded/gate-approved script. Planning delivery: 170–180 spoken words per minute; timing follows the chosen take, never the estimate.

## S01 — The balance that vanished

The Fed shrank its balance sheet by trillions. Bank reserves barely budged.

One reason: another account absorbed much of the adjustment. It once held two and a half trillion dollars. Now it has almost emptied.

That cushion has shrunk.

To follow the next pressure on the economy, watch what happens to the cost of borrowing cash overnight.

## S02 — The economic stakes

A business can have customers, orders, and a profitable product, then get squeezed when its financing comes due.

In this series, we're following the pressures that can break an economy from the inside.

This chapter follows the money between the institutions supplying that financing.

Before you see the hiring freeze or the cancelled expansion, someone has to decide what the next loan will cost.

## S03 — Name the metric

One shortcut for watching this system is called net liquidity.

Take the Fed's assets. Subtract the government's cash account. Subtract the cash parked overnight at the Fed.

Three numbers, one line on a chart.

Analysts build it from public data. The hidden part is what those movements mean—and what the line leaves out.

## S04 — Give the viewer a job

By the end, you'll be able to separate three things: money moving between accounts, funding becoming expensive, and that expense reaching a borrower.

We start with a contradiction.

The Fed can shrink its balance sheet while the banks' reserve balances hold up.

If you only watch the first number, you miss how the system absorbed the change.

## S05 — What the Fed owns

Start with the Fed's balance sheet.

On one side are the assets it owns, largely Treasury securities and mortgage-backed securities.

On the other are its liabilities, including currency, bank reserves, and the government's cash account.

During quantitative tightening, maturing securities can roll off without full replacement. The balance sheet gets smaller.

But the liabilities do not all have to shrink together.

## S06 — Follow a tax payment

Take a tax payment.

Your deposit falls. Your bank settles the payment. The Treasury's balance at the Fed rises.

Holding the other moving parts still, bank reserves fall as the government's cash balance grows.

That government account is the Treasury General Account—the TGA.

Money has moved into a different account. The financial system feels where it landed.

## S07 — Follow the spending back

Now let the government pay a contractor.

The Treasury account falls, the contractor's bank receives reserves, and the contractor receives a deposit.

The direction reverses.

That's why a tax date and a spending date can matter even when the Fed leaves its headline interest rate alone.

And when Treasury raises cash by selling debt, who buys that debt—and how they fund it—also matters.

## S08 — The other account

Then there is overnight reverse repo.

Money market funds can place cash at the Fed overnight against Treasury collateral and earn interest.

They weigh that return against alternatives, including Treasury bills and lending in private repo markets.

Think of this account as an available place for short-term cash to settle.

Its balance tells you where that cash is sitting today.

## S09 — The cushion builds

At the end of twenty twenty-two, that balance briefly reached about two and a half trillion dollars.

Look at the size of it beside today's balance.

The change is enormous. But following the money out of the account matters more than watching the number fall.

It was being moved into other investments, with consequences for the rest of the system.

## S10 — Where it went

As other money-market investments became more attractive, funds shifted toward Treasury bills and private repo lending.

That reallocation helped the system absorb changes elsewhere on the Fed's balance sheet.

It also explains why a falling Fed balance sheet did not automatically mean bank reserves were falling just as fast.

There was another balance available to shrink first.

## S11 — Make the accounting visible

Here's a simplified example.

The Fed's assets fall by a hundred billion dollars.

Over the same interval, overnight reverse repo falls by a hundred billion too. Hold every other balance fixed.

Bank reserves can stay where they were.

The popular net-liquidity formula stays unchanged as well: one subtraction offsets the other.

You can see why watching only the Fed's total assets misses the story.

## S12 — Prove it with the record

The real record gives us a striking version of that contradiction.

By June twenty twenty-five, the Fed reported that its assets had fallen about two point two trillion dollars since runoff began.

Yet bank reserves were slightly higher.

Reverse repo and the Treasury account had both fallen over that period, helping offset the drain on reserves.

The balance sheet shrank. The banks' cushion held.

## S13 — The turn

But a balance that has already fallen close to zero has little room to fall again.

There is less capacity to absorb another adjustment through that account.

A subtraction formula can track balances. It cannot tell you whether the next borrower can obtain funding at a workable price.

The quantity leads us to the question. The price gives us another part of the answer.

## S14 — The overnight transaction

Picture a dealer holding Treasury securities.

The dealer needs cash to finance them. A money market fund lends that cash against the securities, with an agreement to reverse the transaction.

That's repo.

Now more securities need financing, or lenders find better uses for their cash. Borrowers compete harder for funding.

The rate they pay can rise when cash is available elsewhere but isn't reaching this market on the same terms.

## S15 — Update the clock

There's a crucial change to the timeline here.

The Fed ended balance-sheet runoff in December twenty twenty-five and began buying short-term Treasuries to maintain ample reserves.

So today's story has to include those purchases.

They help replenish the system while Treasury cash flows and financing needs keep changing.

The pressure depends on how those forces meet.

## S16 — Put the remaining capacity on screen

In its July twenty twenty-six report, the Fed put bank reserves at roughly three point one trillion dollars and described them as ample.

The small reverse-repo balance sits beside a much larger banking system.

A near-empty account changes the system's flexibility. The next question is how those reserves and the demand for financing meet in the overnight market.

## S17 — Two demands for the same funding

Treasury financing can push on that market from two directions.

More longer-term securities can increase dealers' financing needs.

More Treasury bills can attract money market funds that might otherwise lend through repo.

Fed researchers describe both channels: demand for financing on one side, competing uses for cash on the other.

The same total balance can conceal a different funding problem.

## S18 — Follow the calendar

Then the calendar intervenes.

Taxes arrive. Debt settles. Government spending leaves the Treasury's account.

Each flow changes who holds the cash and when they need it.

Mark the payment date, the settlement date, and the days that follow.

Put those dates beside the funding rates, and you can start testing the story instead of guessing from a market sell-off.

## S19 — Read the price

Start with a secured funding rate such as the tri-party general collateral rate: the price of overnight cash against Treasury collateral.

Compare it with the interest rate the Fed pays banks on reserves.

Watch how that gap behaves through the cash movements we just traced.

Then follow the gap after the event. Has the cash returned to this market, or are borrowers still paying more to obtain it?

## S20 — Check the evidence against the fear

The July report gives us a useful check on the frightening version of this story.

Reverse-repo usage was already close to zero on most days, yet overnight money markets were stable.

The Fed's purchases had accommodated seasonal cash movements.

That is a real counterexample to treating an empty reverse-repo account as an automatic crisis.

The adjustment can work. Our job is to watch whether it continues working.

## S21 — Bring it back to the business

Now return to the business with the loan coming due.

Persistent funding pressure can add to the cost of financing, alongside credit risk, the lender's costs, and the borrower's own condition.

A company that needs to refinance has to face the new terms. It may postpone equipment, hiring, or expansion.

That is how a financial-market strain can become an operating decision.

The route matters as much as the headline.

## S22 — The test that can fail

Here is the test I would use for this part of the story.

After a Treasury cash swing, does the funding-rate gap stay elevated? Do recurring pressures come with greater reliance on the Fed's standing repo facility?

Use of that backstop shows where cash is being supplied; it isn't a failure by itself.

If rates settle and financing stays orderly, this episode's liquidity-stress case has lost its support. We should say so.

## S23 — Give the viewer the payoff

You now have a way to read the next move.

Check which account changed. Check the price of funding. Check whether that pressure persists.

Keep the observation dates together; last month's reserve balance cannot explain today's rate by itself.

And ask whether the borrower you are studying actually needs new financing.

That takes you from a dramatic line on a chart to a question you can investigate.
