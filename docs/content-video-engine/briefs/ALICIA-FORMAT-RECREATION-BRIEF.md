## Narrator voice

First person, earned from the operator's actual biography — the rare
two-altitude view of money:

- **ex-JPMorgan**: watched high earners hold zero margin, up close
- **ex-dispensary owner**: an all-cash business; employed the people finance
  YouTube never talks about; counted the register
- **product manager who builds with AI**: systems beat intentions; money
  treated as a product with a spec

The characters stop being "someone earning $X" and become people from those
two worlds: **the banker** and **the budtender**. The narrator has stood in
both of their apartments — that claim is the hook.

## Script (original, ~560 words, ~3:00 at spoken pace)

**[s01 — black screen, captions punching in word groups]**

Two apartments. I've stood inside both. Same city, same gray carpet in
the hallway. One belongs to a second-year banker pulling ninety-five grand.
The other belongs to a woman who sold weed for me — legally — at
twenty-two dollars an hour. One of them is on track to never need a paycheck
again. It's not the banker.

**[s02 — the banker's apartment]**

I worked at JPMorgan. I know this apartment before the door opens. There's a
suit rack that cost a paycheck, a watch box with a loan inside it, a
television wider than the couch, and a rowing machine holding up laundry.
Half the closet still has tags on. Every object in here was, at some point,
a reward for surviving the week.

**[s03 — coin stack, calendar]**

Now watch the account. Rent clears and the balance flinches. Bonus month is
a memory by March. One bad quarter, one blown transmission, and this whole
apartment runs on a credit card it can't pay off. Ninety-five grand a year —
and no floor under any of it.

**[s04 — the budtender's apartment]**

The budtender makes about half that. Walk in and the first thing you notice
is floor. A couch that gets sat on. Exactly the kitchen tools that get used.
On the table there's a ledger — she kept one on paper — and a jar her tips
pass through on the way to somewhere that pays *her*. Eighteen months of
expenses, sitting quietly. When you count other people's cash for eight hours
a day, you stop worshipping it.

**[s05 — trading desk vs register, bar vs shields]**

I've watched money from both sides of the counter — the trading floor and
the cash register. Run these two side by side: he out-earns her almost two to
one. She holds ten times his margin. Not because she's smarter. Because of
one relationship he never noticed he was in.

**[s06 — host, spec sheet]**

The relationship between stuff and money. These days I'm a product manager —
I build things with AI for a living — and that job beats one lesson into
you: outcomes come from systems, not intentions. Money is a product like any
other, and this video is its spec — the rules the budtender ran without ever
writing them down. Rule one is hiding on every receipt you've ever thrown
away. It's still there at the end of this video, and I'll show you how to
calculate yours.

**[s07 — receipt, second line appears]**

Rule one: every price tag is a down payment. The number on the receipt is
what it costs to *get* the thing. Below it, invisible, is what it costs to
*keep* the thing — space, upkeep, attention. Nobody invoices you for the
second line. You pay it anyway.

**[s08 — rowing machine, rent pipe]**

Take the banker's rowing machine. Say his place runs eighteen hundred a month
for nine hundred square feet — two dollars a square foot, every month. The
machine and its clearance take about twenty square feet. Forty dollars a
month. Nearly five hundred a year in rent, paid to a laundry rack. And
that's one object in one apartment. Now do the whole country.

**[s09 — storage map, callback]**

This country has more self-storage facilities
than fast-food restaurants of the biggest chain `[verify]`. Remember the
banker's spare room? That's a storage unit with better carpet. He pays rent
on it every month. The invoice just never arrives.

**[s10 — host, direct]**

That's your second receipt — and it's the small one. Rule two is what the
budtender does with money the banker never even sees. It isn't "spend less."
She'd tell you spending less is how you stay exactly where you are.
