7. (b12) In oral composition, the position of a word within a sentence dictates its psychological impact6. Scriptwriters utilize the Terminal Stress Principle, which dictates that the most critical, surprising, or emotionally resonant word must be placed at the very end of the sentence6. 22. (b37) The human brain is an efficient pattern-matching engine24. Speechwriting leverages this cognitive reality through the Rule of Three, or tricolon24. Triads create an acoustic rhythm that feels balanced, complete, and memorable24. The first item establishes a baseline, the second extends the pattern, and the third completes the structural unit—or, in comedic and high-impact rhetoric, subverts the established pattern to generate surprise24.
23. (b38) Complementing the triad is Anaphora—the deliberate repetition of a starting word or phrase across successive clauses23. Anaphora establishes a rhythmic acoustic beat that serves as an anchor for complex arguments23. By keeping the opening phrase constant, the listener’s cognitive processing burden is reduced, allowing them to focus entirely on the evolving conceptual information at the end of each clause6.

27. (b43) The Ira Glass Dual-Engine Model
28. (b45) The Anecdote serves as sequential attentional bait31. It consists of a sequence of actions arranged chronologically, detailing what happened in a step-by-step progression32. Human cognition is naturally drawn to sequential stories32. Crucially, an effective anecdote must constantly raise unresolved questions in the listener's mind, building momentum that compels the viewer to stay engaged to find the answer31.
29. (b46) The Reflection provides meaning generation31. An anecdote operating alone is merely a series of disconnected events2. To transform events into a compelling narrative, the writer must periodically interrupt the chronological action to deliver a moment of reflection that answers why the story matters31. The reflection extracts thematic, philosophical, or systemic lessons from the preceding anecdote2.
30. (b47) A script consisting entirely of reflection becomes an abstract, alienating lecture, whereas a script consisting entirely of anecdotes becomes a shallow list of events2. High-performing narrative scripts continuously alternate between these two engines, using the anecdote to capture attention and the reflection to deliver cognitive value31.

32. (b50) * The Martini Glass Structure: The script opens with a sharp, intriguing anecdote that hooks immediate attention34. It then transitions into a linear, chronological narrative body that builds tension toward a central crisis or turning point34. 33. (b51) * The Kabob Structure: The script skewers multiple discrete anecdotal vignettes onto a central thematic thread34.
