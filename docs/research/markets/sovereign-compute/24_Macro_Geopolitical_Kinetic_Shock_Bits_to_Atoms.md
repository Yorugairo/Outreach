## **Behavioral Economics: Kahneman's Prospect Theory and the Options Dilemma**

While the macroeconomic justification for the defense technology rotation appears robust under Dalio's framework, the specific execution strategy proposed by the core research document introduces significant friction when subjected to behavioral analysis. The document advises options traders who are holding heavily underwater three, six, and twelve-month technology call spreads to absolutely hold those spreads rather than liquidating them at a fifty percent loss to fully fund the defense rotation.

The mathematical and quantitative logic presented to support this hold strategy is twofold. First, the document asserts the mathematical reality that realizing a fifty percent loss requires a one hundred percent gain on the remaining capital simply to break even. Second, because technology "spreads" (such as Bull Call Spreads) involve both a long and a short option leg, the short leg actively insulates the trader against the devastating Implied Volatility (IV) crush that notoriously destroys naked options buyers during a market crash. The document argues that capitulating on these spreads mechanically locks in permanent capital destruction right as passive index positioning restricts directional flexibility, thus causing the trader to miss the inevitable "IV recapture" and fundamental mean reversion of technology monopolies that typically occurs six to twelve months after a geopolitical shock.

To rigorously evaluate the validity of this options strategy, the behavioral economic frameworks developed by Nobel laureate Daniel Kahneman and his partner Amos Tversky must be applied. According to Kahneman's foundational *Prospect Theory*, human beings do not make financial decisions based on expected utility in a rational, calculated manner; instead, they are overwhelmingly driven by loss aversion. Psychological studies demonstrate that the emotional pain of losing a specific amount of capital is psychologically twice as powerful as the joy of gaining that exact same amount.

This psychological asymmetry gives rise to the "Disposition Effect," a highly robust market anomaly where investors exhibit a profound, irrational tendency to sell winning assets too early (in order to lock in the psychological reward of a gain) while holding onto losing assets for far too long (in order to avoid the psychological pain of formally realizing a loss). When faced with a severe fifty percent drawdown in a technology options spread, a strictly rational market actor evaluates the immediate opportunity cost: will the remaining capital generate a higher expected return over the next six months if left in the underwater technology spread, or if reallocated into the surging defense sector?

Kahneman's research suggests that the decision to "hold the line" on underwater technology spreads is rarely a product of cold, quantitative mathematical calculation regarding IV crush mitigation. Rather, it is almost exclusively a manifestation of the "sunk cost fallacy" and the "negative hope-fear balance". Traders hold the losing spreads hoping the underlying asset will bounce back, thus continuously deferring the emotional realization of the loss.

If the geopolitical shock of early 2026 truly represents a permanent structural transition from "Bits to Atoms," as the research document simultaneously claims, then holding mid-duration technology options is mathematically and logically irrational. The fundamental underlying assets—such as software hyperscalers reliant on commercial advertising budgets—face severe near-term economic headwinds due to the conflict. The insulation provided by the short leg of a call spread mathematically protects against implied volatility contraction, but it offers absolutely zero protection against a permanent, directional decline in the underlying asset's price. Therefore, the strategy of retaining the underwater tech spreads while synthetically attempting to build a defense barbell is highly likely a behavioral error dressed in quantitative justification, violating the very macroeconomic premise the rotation is based upon.
